Case Study 01 · Credit Risk

Home Credit Risk Analysis

Identifying which loan applicant profiles carry the most default risk, to help a lender make faster, fairer, evidence-based approval decisions.

01

Business Problem

Lenders approving credit on limited criteria run two risks at once: approve too freely and defaults erode portfolio profitability; screen too conservatively and genuinely low-risk applicants get turned away. Home Credit needed a clearer, evidence-based view of which applicant characteristics actually predict default — rather than relying on a single factor like income, which turns out to be a weaker signal on its own than it first appears.

02

Stakeholder Request

The Head of Credit Risk asked for an analysis identifying which applicant profiles carry the most default risk — by income, age, gender, occupation and existing debt burden — so underwriting criteria could be tightened for genuinely high-risk segments without penalising applicants who are statistically low-risk.

03

Business Objectives

  • Establish the scale and quality of the dataset before drawing any conclusions from it.
  • Quantify the overall default rate and confirm how imbalanced the dataset is between defaulters and non-defaulters.
  • Identify which applicant attributes — income, debt-to-income ratio, age, gender and occupation — are most strongly associated with default.
  • Translate the strongest predictors into a simple, explainable rule-based scoring model that supports an Approve / Review / Reject lending decision.
04

Dataset Overview

Source Kaggle — Home Credit Default Risk
Rows 305,545
Columns Confirm from DESCRIBE output
Format CSV → MySQL table

Each row represents one loan application at Home Credit. The TARGET column flags whether the applicant experienced payment difficulties (1) or repaid successfully (0) — SK_ID_CURR confirmed as a unique client identifier with no duplicate records. Fields cover demographics (age via DAYS_BIRTH, gender), financials (AMT_INCOME_TOTAL, AMT_CREDIT, AMT_ANNUITY), employment, occupation, education and family status. The dataset is imbalanced toward successful repayments, which shaped how the analysis was framed — proportions and rates rather than raw counts.

05

Data Cleaning

1

Schema & structure review

Counted total records, ran DESCRIBE application_train to review column names and types, and previewed sample rows to understand the data format before writing any analysis queries.

2

Uniqueness check

Confirmed COUNT(*) matched COUNT(DISTINCT SK_ID_CURR) — no duplicate client records in the dataset.

3

Missing value checks

Verified AMT_INCOME_TOTAL had zero missing values, making it reliable for financial analysis; filtered out NULL and blank OCCUPATION_TYPE values before analysing default rate by occupation.

06

Analysis Process

1

Default distribution

Measured the split between defaulters and non-defaulters to confirm the dataset is imbalanced toward successful repayments — the baseline every other cut of the data was compared against.

2

Financial profile comparison

Compared average income, credit amount and annuity between defaulters and non-defaulters, then segmented applicants into Low / Middle / High income bands and Low / Moderate / High debt-to-income bands.

3

Demographic risk analysis

Broke default rate down by age band × gender, and separately by occupation type, to find which applicant profiles carry the most risk.

4

Rule-based risk scoring model

Built a CTE-based scoring model combining age, income and credit-burden risk into a single 3–9 point score, translated into an Approve / Review / Reject lending decision — a transparent, explainable alternative to a black-box model.

08

Key Insights

Insight 01

Income has a protective effect mainly at the top: high-income applicants default at 6.9%, versus 8.22% (low income) and 8.52% (middle income) — a threshold effect, not a gradual one.

Insight 02

Debt burden alone doesn't predict default: the highest debt-to-income group defaults at 8.08% — lower than the moderate-risk group at 8.27%. Loan size relative to income isn't sufficient on its own.

Insight 03

Age and gender combine into the sharpest risk signal: applicants under 30 who are male default at 12.60%, nearly triple applicants over 60 who are female, at 4.55%.

Insight 04

Occupation is a strong risk marker: low-skill labourers default at 17.23% — more than three times the rate of accountants, at 4.85%.

09

Business Impact

Because income and debt burden alone are weak, inconsistent predictors, a lender relying on them in isolation risks approving high-risk applicants at standard terms while over-pricing genuinely low-risk ones — a pattern consistent with the reckless-lending cases seen in debt counselling, where high loan amounts were approved despite limited repayment capacity. Age, gender and occupation showed far larger, more consistent gaps, meaning a lender that weights those factors correctly can tighten screening on the true high-risk segments — young male applicants, low-skill occupations, elevated debt burden — without losing the low-risk customers a purely income-based filter would wrongly flag.

10

Recommendations

01

Apply stricter credit assessment for young (under-30) male applicants and low-skill occupation categories, where default rates run 2–3x the portfolio average.

02

Don't rely on debt-to-income ratio alone as a risk filter — combine it with income band, age and occupation, since high debt burden alone did not correspond to the highest default rate.

03

Offer more competitive terms to demonstrably lower-risk segments — older female applicants and professional occupations like accounting — to stay competitive without adding risk.

04

Adopt the rule-based Approve / Review / Reject scoring logic as a fast, transparent first-pass screen — one that's easy to explain to both underwriters and regulators, ahead of any future statistical model.
