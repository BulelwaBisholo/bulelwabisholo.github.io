Customer Churn Analysis

Identifying which South African bank customers are most likely to churn — and why — so the retention team can act before they leave, not after.

01

Business Problem

South African retail banks lose revenue every time a customer closes an account or moves to a competitor — and by the time attrition shows up in a high-level retention report, it's often too late to intervene. The retention team could see that customers were leaving, but had no systematic way to see which customers were most likely to leave next, or why.

02

Stakeholder Request

The Head of Customer Retention asked for an analysis that went beyond a single "churn rate" number: which customer profiles were actually driving attrition, and whether those patterns held true across the bank's different provinces, employment segments and product lines — so the retention team could prioritise outreach where it would have the most impact, instead of treating every at-risk customer the same way.

03

Business Objectives

  • Quantify the overall customer churn rate across the full 15,000-customer base.
  • Identify which demographic and financial factors — province, gender, age, employment status, credit score, tenure, product count and account balance — are most strongly associated with churn.
  • Combine the strongest predictors into a single, composite risk score that segments customers by how likely they are to churn.
  • Package the findings into an interactive Power BI dashboard the retention team can filter and explore without needing to write SQL themselves.
04

Dataset Overview

Source Synthetic (self-generated)
Rows 15,000
Columns 15
Format CSV

Each row represents one South African retail banking customer, combining demographics (age, gender, province, education level), financial profile (account balance, estimated salary, credit score), relationship data (tenure, number of products held, credit card ownership) and the churn outcome. The dataset was synthesised in Google Colab (Python — Pandas & NumPy), with churn logic deliberately embedded around employment status, credit score, account balance, product count and tenure — so the analysis could focus on uncovering those relationships.

Data limitation: the Bank field was randomly assigned during data generation and has no real relationship with churn. Any apparent differences between banks are statistical noise, so a bank-level churn comparison was intentionally excluded from this analysis.

05

Data Cleaning

1

Schema & load

Created the customers table in MySQL with explicit data types across all 15 fields, and set CustomerID as the primary key.

2

Missing value audit

Ran a column-by-column NULL check across every field (SUM(CASE WHEN ... IS NULL ...)) to confirm data completeness before starting the analysis.

3

Duplicate check

Grouped by CustomerID and filtered for COUNT(*) > 1 to confirm every customer record was unique.

06

Analysis Process

1

Baseline churn rate

Calculated the overall churn rate across all 15,000 customers as a starting benchmark for every deeper cut of the data.

2

Demographic drivers

Broke churn down by province & gender, employment status, and age band to check whether "who the customer is" predicts churn.

3

Financial & behavioural drivers

Broke churn down by credit score band, tenure & number of products, and account balance tier to check whether "the customer's financial relationship with the bank" predicts churn.

4

Multi-factor risk segmentation

Used a CTE to combine the five strongest individual factors (unemployed, poor credit score, low balance, single product, short tenure) into one composite 0–5 risk score, then grouped customers into No / Low / High / Critical Risk segments.

07

Dashboard & Visualisations

The report is a single interactive page: three KPI cards (15K Total Customers, 3K Churned Customers, 18.47% Churn Rate) at the top, four slicers on the left (Bank, Education Level, Gender, Employment Status), and five charts breaking down churn rate by Age Group, Credit Score Group, Province, Balance Group and Product — plus a summary table cross-referencing Credit Score Group and Balance Group, and a written Key Insights panel.

08

Key Insights

Insight 01

Overall churn rate across the customer base is 18.47%.

Insight 02

Customers with poor credit scores and account balances below R10,000 have the highest churn rate of any segment, at 40.24%.

Insight 03

Low account balance drives elevated churn even among customers with otherwise good credit — it isn't only a "bad credit" problem.

Insight 04

Credit score and account balance emerged as the two strongest individual predictors of churn, ahead of demographic factors like age or province.

Insight 05

The composite 5-factor risk score shows a near-perfect relationship with churn: customers with 0 risk factors never churned, while customers with all 5 factors always churned.

09

Business Impact

Because the composite risk score separates churn so cleanly — 0 risk factors means the customer never churns, 5 means they always do — the retention team can use it as an early-warning flag instead of reacting after a customer has already left. That turns a limited retention budget into a targeted one: effort goes to the "Critical Risk" segment, where churn is both most likely and most preventable, rather than being spread evenly across a customer base where most accounts were never at risk.

10

Recommendations

01

Prioritise retention outreach on the "Critical Risk" segment (4–5 risk factors) first — for this group, churn is near-certain without intervention.

02

Target low-balance customers specifically, not just customers with poor credit — low balance drives churn on its own, so a credit-score-only view of risk would miss this group entirely.

03

Use credit score and account balance as the primary filters in any future retention model, rather than weighting all customer attributes equally.

04

Exclude bank-level comparisons from retention strategy — this analysis confirmed the "Bank" field carries no real signal in this dataset, so it shouldn't drive decisions.

11

Technologies Used

Google Colab (Python)
SQL (MySQL)
Power BI

Google Colab (Python — Pandas & NumPy) was used to synthesise the source dataset. MySQL was used for the full analysis — schema design, data quality checks, and every churn-driver query including the multi-factor risk segmentation. Power BI was used to turn those findings into an interactive, filterable dashboard for the retention team.
